Chapter 1: Safety and Working Around Electricity

Question 1
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: The let-go threshold, roughly 10 to 20 milliamperes, is the point where involuntary muscle contraction keeps the hand on the conductor. The perception threshold is far lower, about 1 milliampere, and produces only a faint tingle that the person can easily pull away from. A ground-fault threshold belongs to protective devices such as GFCIs, which trip at about 4 to 6 milliamperes, and it describes a device setting, not a body response. Arc ionization depends on voltage and gap distance and has nothing to do with muscle response in a hand.

Why the other choices are wrong: Current equals voltage divided by resistance, so 120 divided by 2,000 gives 0.06 ampere, or 60 milliamperes. That value is within the range, roughly 50 to 100 milliamperes, where the heart can go into fibrillation if the path crosses the chest. A result of 6 milliamperes comes from misplacing the decimal point by a factor of ten. A result of 600 milliamperes is ten times too large, and breakers do not protect people anyway because they trip at many amperes. The 0.6 milliampere figure is a hundred times too small.

Chapter 2: Atoms, Charge and Electron Flow

Question 1
Correct answer: C.
Why the other choices are wrong: In a metal conductor, the moving charge carriers are free electrons, which carry a negative charge and sit in the outer region of the atom. Protons are positive but are held in the nucleus by strong forces and do not travel along a wire. Neutrons carry no charge, so they cannot make up a current. The nucleus as a whole stays fixed in the metal's structure, so it does not drift when voltage is applied.
Question 2
Correct answer: D.
Why the other choices are wrong: Copper has a single electron in its outermost shell, and since it is far from the nucleus and weakly held, it breaks free easily, making copper a good conductor. Four valence electrons describes silicon and carbon, which are semiconductors or insulating solids. A full shell of eight describes inert gases such as neon, which do not conduct. Aluminum has three valence electrons, and although it conducts, it does so about 60 percent as well as copper, so the two are not identical.
Question 3
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: Charge equals current multiplied by time, so 4 amperes for 30 seconds gives 120 coulombs, since one ampere is one coulomb each second. Dividing time by current gives 7.5, which has units of seconds per ampere and is not a charge. Adding the two numbers mixes unlike units and has no physical meaning. Dividing current by time yields 0.133 ampere per second, which describes a rate of change in current rather than a quantity of charge.
Question 4
Correct answer: A.
Why the other choices are wrong: Conventional current flow shows current leaving the positive terminal and returning to the negative terminal, and this is the common drawing style in schematics and codes. Electron flow is the opposite direction, because electrons leave the negative terminal and travel toward the positive one. Alternating flow is a property of AC supplies and is not what a battery-fed circuit shows. Static means charge that is not moving, so it does not describe a drawing of current through a load.
Question 5

Why the other choices are wrong: Two coulombs hold twice as many electrons as one coulomb, so 2 × 6.24 × 10^18 equals 12.48 × 10^18, which is written as about 1.25 × 10^19. The value of 6.24 × 10^18 ignores the factor of two and gives the count for a single coulomb. Dividing by two halves the count instead of doubling it. The value of 1.25 × 10^18 is ten times too small, because moving the decimal point one place the wrong way changes the power of ten.
Question 6
Correct answer: B.
Why the other choices are wrong: The ampere is the rate of flow of charge, and one ampere equals one coulomb passing a point in one second. Energy delivered at one joule per second is a watt, which measures power. A volt across an ohm does produce one ampere, but that is a statement of Ohm's law and not the definition of the unit. A single electron per second would be an almost unmeasurably small current, about 1.6 × 10^-19 ampere.
Question 7

Friction does not create charge, since it only moves electrons from one object to another, and the two objects end up with opposite charges. The pair together stays neutral overall, but each object individually is charged, so describing both as neutral overlooks the separation that rubbing produced.
Question 8

A thermocouple depends on temperature difference at a metal junction. A solar cell uses light striking a semiconductor to free charge. A quartz igniter uses mechanical pressure on a crystal, which is the piezoelectric effect. None of those three involves a chemical reaction, so each fits a different voltage source.
Question 9

Placing copper ahead of silver reverses the top two. The last list is nearly the reverse of the correct order.

Resistance is the opposition that holds current back and is measured in ohms, so it works against the push rather than providing it. Conductance is the reciprocal of resistance and describes how easily current passes, using the siemens, which makes it another property of the path and not the push.
